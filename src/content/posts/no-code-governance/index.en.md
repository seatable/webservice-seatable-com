No-code platforms enable the departments in your company to **build digital applications themselves, without relying on classic software development for every project**. Without a binding structure, however, this freedom can quickly lead to [shadow IT]({{< relref "posts/schatten-it" >}}), security gaps and compliance risks. Well-thought-out no-code governance therefore creates a framework in which your employees can work independently while sensitive data, access rights and critical processes remain protected.

## What does no-code governance mean in a company?

No-code governance refers to the **organizational and technical rules with which you manage the use of [no-code platforms]({{< relref "/" >}}) in your company**. You define which platforms are approved, how applications are developed and released, and which security requirements apply. The goal is to allow your departments to work independently while ensuring that applications are used in a controlled and traceable way.

You need to bring several areas together. With IT governance, you create the overarching framework for the use of your IT systems. IT security protects data, applications and [infrastructure]({{< relref "posts/it-infrastruktur" >}}). Through IT compliance, you ensure that you meet internal and external requirements. Data governance also plays an important role when you define how your employees may store, use and share data.

## Why a lack of no-code governance becomes a security risk

No-code makes the development of [applications]({{< relref "posts/20250318-app-erstellen" >}}) much more accessible. But this is exactly where the risk lies. Where employees can set up digital solutions independently of IT, structures quickly emerge that the company can hardly oversee completely.

### Shadow IT and a lack of transparency

When a department urgently needs a solution for a new process, a quickly set-up tool seems practical at first. However, if more and more teams use applications on their own and store sensitive data in them, you quickly lose **oversight**.

It then becomes hard for your IT to tell which platforms are in use, where information is stored and who is responsible for individual applications. This creates **not only shadow IT, but often also data silos**, because information is spread across different systems.

With effective no-code governance, you create **transparency about the platforms and applications in use**. A central directory helps you record existing solutions and identify uncontrolled individual solutions at an early stage.

### Security risks from access and integrations

Even with an approved platform, you significantly influence IT security through **access rights**. If employees can see or edit more data than they need for their tasks, you unnecessarily increase the risk.

With a rights and roles concept, you define which users may access certain applications and information. The **principle of least privilege** gives you clear guidance here: you only grant the permissions a user actually needs for their tasks.

As systems become more interconnected, you should also keep an eye on **API interfaces and external services**. Even an application that is securely configured internally can create risks if it automatically transmits data to third parties. You should therefore firmly integrate cloud security and the control of such connections into your IT security.

![Mouse pointer hovering over the word Security.](it-security.png)

### Compliance and data protection risks

Particular care is required when your applications process personal or other sensitive information. You must be able to trace **which data is stored, what purpose it serves and which systems it is passed on to**.

This is not just about GDPR compliance. Depending on your industry and company structure, you must also consider internal requirements, industry-specific regulations or standards such as ISO 27001. Without proper documentation, this makes IT compliance harder for you, as well as the later analysis of [security incidents]({{< relref "posts/20250612-incident-management" >}}) or faulty changes. You should therefore define when planning an application which data may be processed and which protective measures are required. 

## The most important building blocks of secure no-code governance

To scale [citizen development]({{< relref "posts/20250317-citizen-developer" >}}) securely in your company, you need traceable structures. These should give your employees guidance in their day-to-day work without causing unnecessary administrative effort.

### Define roles and responsibilities

**Define who is responsible for developing, reviewing and maintaining your no-code applications.** Citizen developers can, for example, create and maintain applications, while business owners assess the functional requirements. Your IT administrators take care of the technical framework. For applications with higher protection needs, you should also involve the responsible people from IT security or  IT compliance.

**Document the responsibilities for every productive application.** This way, your employees know whom to contact about changes, technical problems or security questions.

**Also take staff changes into account.** When employees leave your company or change departments, responsibility for their applications must pass to someone else. Define a binding handover process so that important applications do not depend on individual employees.

![A man works at a computer on a desk.](governance-und-compliance.png)

### Classify applications by risk

Not every no-code application places the same demands on your IT security. A simple internal [task list]({{< relref "posts/to-do-liste-online" >}}) needs far less control than an application with which you manage personal [customer data]({{< relref "posts/no-code-crm" >}}) or business-critical processes.

You should therefore classify your applications according to their risk and protection needs. Consider, among other things, the **type of data processed, the impact of possible errors and the connected systems**. On this basis, you determine which security requirements apply and which bodies you involve in the review. This way, you align your controls with the actual risk and deploy your resources where they matter most.

### Manage rights and access centrally

With a well-thought-out rights and roles concept, you prevent employees from gaining access to data they do not need for their tasks. Assign permissions as granularly as possible and review them regularly.

Make sure you do not only define access rights when building an application. Teams change, employees take on new tasks and additional functions extend existing applications. Check regularly whether the rights granted still match the current process. With central **access control**, you keep an overview and can more easily maintain a consistent level of IT security.

![Wooden figures representing a hierarchy.](administration-und-berechtigungen.png)

### Establish uniform IT security processes

You do not have to develop security checks from scratch for every application. With standardized IT security processes, you create a fixed procedure for recurring reviews. Depending on the use case, you should consider the following points, among others:

- the review of **processed data and its protection needs**
- the control of **access rights and roles**
- the assessment of **connected systems and API interfaces**
- securing **authentication**
- secure **data storage**
- the planning of **backups and recovery procedures**

To do this, apply your existing IT security policies to the use of no-code applications. Uniform reviews and regular check-ups give your departments guidance and at the same time reduce the coordination effort for your IT security team.

## Challenges when introducing no-code governance

Introducing no-code governance requires more than setting binding rules. Companies must integrate the requirements into existing workflows and make sure the teams involved apply them in everyday work. Several challenges often arise in the process:

- limited staff and time **[IT resources]({{< relref "posts/it-fachkraeftemangel" >}}) for reviews and controls**
- **rapid changes** in the no-code platforms in use
- **different requirements and ways of working** across departments
- **lack of acceptance** of additional rules

It is particularly important to make the rules understandable and practical. If the requirements are too complicated or involve a lot of effort, your employees become less willing to follow them consistently. In doing so, you encourage exactly the shadow IT you actually want to avoid. You should therefore involve the affected departments early and align the governance processes with their actual workflows.

## What governance can look like across the entire application process

It is best to integrate your governance requirements directly into the entire application process. Do not wait until shortly before an application is released. Define right from the start which requirements apply during development, review, use and later decommissioning.

{{< accordion "Step 1: Development" >}}

Give your citizen developers a clear framework during development. This includes, for example, that they only use approved platforms and permitted data sources.<br><br/>Word your IT security policies so clearly that your employees can see for themselves which requirements apply to their use case. This way, you take security aspects into account directly during development and avoid later corrections.

{{< /accordion >}}


{{< accordion "Step 2: Review and approval" >}}

Once the application has been developed, check whether it meets the defined requirements. Use the previously determined risk category and the requirements that apply to it as your guide.<br><br/>Document the review result and record who approved the application. For changes that significantly affect the risk profile, you should provide for a new review. This keeps it traceable on what basis an application may be used in production.

{{< /accordion >}}


{{< accordion "Step 3: Operation and monitoring" >}}

Keep an eye on your applications even after approval. During operation, new users are added, permissions change and teams extend existing functions.<br><br/>With an audit trail, you can trace relevant activities and more easily follow changes to data or systems. This helps you with internal reviews as well as with analyzing potential problems.<br><br/>Also check regularly whether access rights, integrations and responsibilities still match the current purpose of use.

{{< /accordion >}}


{{< accordion "Step 4: Archiving and decommissioning" >}}

End the life cycle of a no-code application in a controlled way as soon as it is no longer needed. First check whether other processes or systems still access the application. Back up or archive required data in line with your retention policies and delete information that is no longer needed, unless legal or internal requirements prevent this.<br><br/>Then remove access rights that are no longer needed and deactivate connected interfaces. Document the decommissioning so that it remains clear when the application was taken out of operation and which measures were taken.

{{< /accordion >}}

## How to resolve the conflict between IT security and agility in your company

You may worry that additional governance rules will slow down the benefits of citizen development again. If your employees have to go through several approval stages for every small change, you do lose part of the speed that no-code is actually meant to offer. What matters is therefore **adjusting the scope of controls to the respective risk**.

So create clear room for maneuver for your citizen developers. Set **binding standards** and enable **independent decisions** within this framework. Standardized review procedures and clearly defined responsibilities help make coordination more efficient.

What matters is that your IT governance does not centralize every decision, but only provides for additional controls where they are necessary. This way, you combine reliable IT security processes with flexible development processes and enable your departments to implement new solutions quickly.

## Conclusion: Give no-code a framework with governance

With no-code, you give your departments the opportunity to shape digital processes independently. Without suitable structures, however, you risk shadow IT, unclear responsibilities and additional security gaps.

With effective no-code governance, you combine organizational requirements with technical safeguards. You define roles, classify applications by risk, manage access rights and establish uniform IT security processes.

Do not see governance as a rigid set of rules. Platforms, processes and requirements keep evolving. So review your structures regularly and adapt them to new requirements. This way, you can benefit from no-code and keep your IT security in view in the long term.

## FAQs on no-code governance

{{< faq "What is meant by no-code governance in a corporate context?" >}}
With no-code governance, you define the organizational and technical rules for using no-code platforms in your company. You determine which tools your employees may use, who can develop applications and which requirements apply to approval, operation and control. This connects citizen development with your IT governance, IT security and IT compliance.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How does no-code governance prevent shadow IT from emerging?" >}}
You avoid shadow IT above all when you offer your departments a practical, approved alternative. Provide suitable platforms and define clear rules for their use. This allows your employees to build their own solutions without resorting to unauthorized applications. Your IT retains an overview of the systems in use, responsibilities and data flows.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What influence does no-code governance have on IT security?" >}}
With no-code governance, you take IT security requirements into account during development. You check access rights, data flows, interfaces and risk categories early on, and with uniform IT security processes you create a fixed framework for recurring controls. This reduces risks from overly broad permissions, insecure integrations or uncontrolled data sharing.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Doesn't strict IT governance hold back the innovation of citizen developers?" >}}
That depends on how you design your requirements. If every application has to go through the same extensive approval process, you can indeed slow down development. A risk-based approach creates more flexibility. Give simple applications more freedom and review sensitive applications more thoroughly. This keeps your departments able to act, and you focus your security measures on the areas that actually need them.
{{< /faq >}}
